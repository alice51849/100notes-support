How to take handwritten Cornell notes on iPad or iPhone

Cornell notes work because they split one page into three jobs: capture during class, ask yourself questions afterwards, and sum up the page in a few lines. Writing them by hand on an iPad or iPhone keeps the thinking of pen and paper and adds what paper cannot do: searchable handwriting, lecture slides on the same page, and flashcards for review. Here is a routine you can follow from the first lecture to the exam.

The Cornell page in one minute

A Cornell page has three areas:

AreaWhereWhat goes in it
NotesThe large right-hand columnWhat you hear and see in class: key ideas, definitions, examples, diagrams, in your own short words
CuesA narrow left-hand column, about a third of the widthQuestions and keywords you add after class, one next to each chunk of notes
SummaryA strip across the bottomTwo to four sentences: what this page was about, in your words

The layout matters less than the habit: leave the cue column empty during class and come back to it the same day. That second pass is where most of the learning happens.

Before class: set up the page

  1. One notebook per course. Name it after the course and keep the pages in date order, so a whole semester reads like a paper notebook.
  2. Draw the three areas once. Use a ruler line for the cue column and another for the summary strip. In 100 Notes Studio you can draw them with the ruler and shape tools, then use the lasso to copy the lines onto the next page instead of drawing them again.
  3. Bring the slides onto the page. If your lecturer shares slides as a PDF, import it so you can write on the slides themselves, or keep the PDF in a reference view beside your handwritten page.
  4. Write the date and topic at the top. It costs five seconds and makes search and review much easier later.

During class: capture, do not transcribe

  • Write in short phrases and abbreviations, not full sentences. If you are writing every word, you are not choosing what matters.
  • Leave a blank line between topics, so each chunk can get its own cue later.
  • Draw quick diagrams instead of describing them. Arrows, boxes and a sketch of the graph on the board are faster than words.
  • Mark anything you did not understand with a “?” in the margin and keep going.
  • If recording is allowed in your course, an audio recording attached to the page lets you replay the part you missed instead of guessing.

After class: cues, summary and flashcards

  1. Same day, fill the cue column. For each chunk of notes, write a question the chunk answers: “What are the three causes of…?”, “Define opportunity cost.”
  2. Write the summary strip. Two to four sentences, without looking at the notes first, then check.
  3. Turn the key cues into flashcards. Each cue is already a question; the notes next to it are the answer. Use spaced repetition so the cards you know come back less often and the ones you miss come back sooner.
  4. Before the exam, cover the notes column. Read each cue and answer out loud. Anything you cannot answer goes on your review list.
